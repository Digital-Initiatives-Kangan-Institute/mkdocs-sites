# Safety Checks for Power and Test Instruments

The Laboratory Test Equipment section above covered *how* a DMM, power supply, oscilloscope, and the rest are used to measure and test a circuit. This section is about what happens *before* any of that: the checks a competent operator runs on the instrument itself, every time, before it is ever connected to a live circuit. This is not a formality: an instrument is only as trustworthy as its last inspection, and skipping that inspection is how a tool meant to provide safety becomes a source of risk instead.

<p align="center"><img src="../../../../assets/study-guide/09-power-instrument-safety-checks.png" alt="A three-step pre-use safety sequence for a power instrument: visual inspection, confirming its rated range, then connecting to a live circuit, with a callout on why &quot;it worked last time&quot; is not a valid safety check" width="700"></p>

**Why a rated-range and damage check has to happen *every* time, not just the first time.** It is tempting to assume that if an instrument worked safely yesterday, it remains safe today, but that reasoning skips over exactly the kind of failure that matters most: damage, internal wear, or a developing fault can occur *between* uses, with no visible external sign at all. A lead's insulation can crack from repeated flexing, an internal fuse can fail, or a connector can loosen, none of which announce themselves until the instrument is put back into use. Treating "it worked last time" as sufficient evidence of safety is the assumption that real workplace procedures are designed to prevent.

**A concrete, two-part pre-use check:**
1. **Visual inspection**: look over the instrument's leads, probes, and outer casing for any sign of physical damage: cracks, exposed conductors, frayed insulation, or a damaged connector.
2. **Confirm the rated range**: check that the instrument's rated voltage and current range genuinely *exceeds* what the circuit under test could deliver. Using an instrument rated below the circuit's actual voltage or current is itself a hazard, independent of whether the instrument is otherwise undamaged. This is why bench multimeters and clamp meters carry a **CAT rating** (CAT I–IV), a category that indicates which part of an electrical installation the instrument is actually safe to be connected to, not just what maximum voltage number is printed on the dial.

Only once both of these checks pass is it appropriate to connect the instrument to a live circuit. Skipping either step trades a few seconds of inspection for a real risk of equipment damage, a short circuit, or personal injury. None of this depends on what is being tested: the same two checks apply whether the instrument is about to be connected to an ESP32 dev board's 3.3 V rail or a mains-powered industrial control cabinet; only the consequences of getting it wrong scale with the voltage involved (see the Workplace Electrical Equipment Range section below).
