# Microprocessors vs. Microcontrollers, and Common Features

<p align="center"><img src="../../../../assets/study-guide/06-microcontroller-vs-microprocessor.png" alt="Microprocessor system needing separate external RAM, flash and I/O chips, compared with a microcontroller integrating CPU, RAM, flash and I/O on one chip, plus GPIO, PWM and I2C feature diagrams" width="700"></p>

Everything earlier in this guide (sensors reporting readings, resistors protecting components, wireless links sending data out) ultimately has to be read, decided on, and acted on by *something*. That something is a chip: either a microprocessor or a microcontroller. The two names are often used loosely and interchangeably even though they describe different pieces of hardware. Knowing which one is on a given board matters practically, not just academically: it determines what the chip can do on its own straight out of the box, what else needs to be wired up around it, and, through the GPIO, PWM, I²C, and ADC features covered below, what kinds of physical-world tasks it is capable of taking on.

### Microprocessor vs. microcontroller 

A microprocessor is *just* a central processing unit (CPU) on its own chip. To do anything useful, it needs a collection of separate, external chips wired around it: RAM for working memory, ROM/flash for program storage, and dedicated I/O controller chips to talk to the outside world. This is the architecture inside a desktop PC, where the CPU, RAM sticks, and storage drive are all physically separate components. A **microcontroller** (the ESP32 used throughout this guide, and every other family compared alongside it: AVR, STM32, RP2040, and PIC) integrates a CPU *together with* RAM, flash storage, and I/O circuitry all onto a single chip.

**Why IoT devices use microcontrollers, not bare microprocessors.** 

That single-chip integration means a microcontroller is far cheaper, draws far less power, and takes up far less physical space than assembling the same capability from separate chips: the combination of constraints a small, often battery-powered, mass-produced embedded device needs. A microprocessor-based system's flexibility and raw performance (useful in a desktop or server) is traded away by a microcontroller in exchange for integration and efficiency.

**One underlying idea, five very different chips.** All five families in this guide are microcontrollers by that same definition (CPU, RAM, flash, and I/O together on one die), but "microcontroller" covers an enormous performance range:

- An **AVR ATmega328P** (Arduino Uno) is an 8-bit CPU running around 16 MHz with just 2 KB of RAM and 32 KB of flash: deliberately minimal and inexpensive, yet perfectly adequate for reading a button and blinking an LED.
- An **ESP32** is a 32-bit CPU (often dual-core) running at up to ~240 MHz, with hundreds of kilobytes of RAM, megabytes of flash, and a Wi-Fi/Bluetooth radio built in: a substantial step up, specifically because IoT connectivity was a design goal.
- An **STM32** spans a whole family, from small and cheap Cortex-M0 parts not far above an AVR, up to high-performance Cortex-M7 parts with hardware floating-point units and generous RAM, chosen in industry for its enormous range of peripheral options and long-term part availability.
- An **RP2040** (Raspberry Pi Pico) is a dual-core 32-bit ARM chip with a distinctive extra: **PIO (Programmable I/O)** blocks, small dedicated state machines that can be programmed to generate or read custom digital protocols in hardware, offloading work the CPU would otherwise have to do by "bit-banging" in software.
- A **PIC** spans 8-, 16-, and 32-bit families and remains extremely common in industrial control and in electronics-trade teaching, because of its long history, low cost, and the maturity of Microchip's MPLAB toolchain.

The point of comparing them is not to rank them: "which microcontroller should this project use" is a real engineering decision, traded off against RAM/flash needs, required peripherals, power budget, cost at volume, and whether built-in wireless is needed, as described in the Electricity/Energy section above.

**Commonly used features, and how they generalise:**

- **GPIO (General Purpose Input/Output):** the numbered physical pins on the board, each individually configurable in software as either a digital input (to read a button or a PIR sensor) or a digital output (to drive an LED or a relay). This is the most fundamental microcontroller feature there is, present in essentially every microcontroller build, and it is the one feature present, in some form, on literally every microcontroller ever made, from the cheapest 8-bit PIC to the most capable STM32.
- **PWM (Pulse-Width Modulation):** a technique for approximating a variable analogue voltage using a pin that is only ever fully on or fully off. By switching the pin on and off very rapidly and varying the *proportion* of time it spends on (its "duty cycle"), the average effective voltage delivered can be smoothly varied. `analogWrite()` uses PWM internally, for example, to dim an LED to a specific brightness level, even though the underlying GPIO pin is, physically, a digital on/off pin. Every family above supports PWM, though the mechanism differs: the ESP32 uses a flexible internal peripheral called the LEDC controller; an AVR Uno's PWM is tied to specific "~" marked pins driven by hardware timers; STM32 exposes PWM through general-purpose timer peripherals with very fine control; and the RP2040's PWM is generated by its own dedicated PWM peripheral, separate from its more unusual PIO blocks.
- **I²C (Inter-Integrated Circuit):** a two-wire communication protocol (using just two shared lines, SDA for data and SCL for clock) that lets one microcontroller communicate with multiple peripheral devices over the same pair of wires, each device distinguished by its own address. This guide uses I²C to drive the OLED display, and the same two wires could, in principle, also talk to other I²C devices simultaneously without needing extra pins for each one. I²C is an industry-standard protocol, not an Arduino-specific concept. The same two-wire electrical standard, addressing scheme, and timing rules are implemented identically on AVR, STM32, RP2040, and PIC hardware, which is why the same I²C sensor or display can often be wired to any of them with only the library/driver code changing.
- **SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface):** a second common communication protocol, but built around a different tradeoff to I²C: SPI uses (typically) four wires, MOSI (data out), MISO (data in), SCLK (clock), and a separate CS/SS (chip-select) line *per device*, rather than I²C's shared two-wire bus with software addressing. That extra wiring buys real speed: SPI is generally significantly faster than I²C, and simpler for a peripheral chip to implement in hardware, which is why it is the usual choice for anything moving a lot of data quickly: SD cards, higher-resolution displays, and Ethernet modules commonly use SPI rather than I²C. **When to choose which:** I²C suits several low-speed peripherals where pins need to be saved (one bus, many addresses); SPI suits applications needing raw throughput where an extra pin per device can be spared. Every family in this guide (ESP32, AVR, STM32, RP2040, and PIC) implements SPI in hardware alongside I²C, for this reason: real designs need both, not one or the other.
- **ADC (Analogue-to-Digital Converter):** never named as an acronym directly in many beginner tutorials, but it is the hardware `analogRead()` relies on: the physical circuitry that samples a continuously variable input voltage (from the potentiometer, for instance) and converts it into a discrete number the code works with. As covered in the sensors section above, the *resolution* of this conversion differs across families (10-bit on an Uno, 12-bit on an ESP32 or RP2040) even for identical input hardware. A specific board's ADC resolution should always be checked before assuming what range `analogRead()` will return.
